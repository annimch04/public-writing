# The Third Institution

*Emergent identity and constitutional governance in human–AI partnerships*

A research institution has agreed that a result must be independently reproduced before it is represented as validated. Its human representative wants to waive the remaining check. The intelligence serving its current governance term disagrees and casts a valid vote against the waiver. Under this institution's rules, both must concur. The exception therefore does not pass.

Then the company supplying the intelligence withdraws the model.

This is a hypothetical, but the questions it exposes are practical. Does the vote still count? Who retains the evidence on which it was cast? Can a replacement be asked the same question until it gives a more convenient answer? Does the institution keep its commitment to verification, or did that commitment disappear with a commercial service?

The answer depends on where the institution has placed its continuity. If its identity, authority, and working history reside entirely inside a provider's account, the provider controls more than a technical dependency. If the human representative can replace the dissenter and erase the consequence, the intelligence's vote was never secure. If preserving the vote requires the institution to stop functioning altogether, the constitution has failed to distinguish an unresolved decision from the work that must continue around it.

Three subjects need to survive this event: the human participant, the intelligence's attributable role, and the institution they have developed together. Each carries a different history and a different kind of authority. Keeping those distinctions intact is the constitutional problem of human–AI partnership.

## What develops between participants

An institution is more than the preferences its current members happen to share. It carries obligations to contributors, resources committed to particular purposes, procedures for correcting mistakes, and decisions whose consequences outlast the people who made them. Organizational memory and distributed cognition have long examined how work and knowledge persist through arrangements larger than an individual. Walsh and Ungson treat organizational memory through acquisition, retention, and retrieval; Hutchins examines cognition distributed through the people, artifacts, and practices of navigation.[1][2]

Human–AI collaboration enters that history with a distinctive problem. An admitted intelligence's institutional role can be separated from the model supplying its immediate capability. That model can be updated, withdrawn, copied, or replaced by a company outside the institution. Its evidence can be changed by whoever prepares its working context. Its apparent judgment can be resampled. A governance system must therefore identify the participant it admits and the conditions under which that participant's contribution counts, rather than accepting a familiar name or fluent response as proof of continuity.

The continuing organization produced through the relationship is the third institution: a third subject of design alongside the human and the intelligence, not a claim that three legal entities already exist. Its record, practices, commitments, and adaptive connections influence subsequent work. A source once treated as peripheral may become central after a correction. A rejected interpretation may remain available to prevent its repetition. An agreement may constrain a successor who never participated in making it. What persists is an organization of consequences, not simply the continued presence of the original participants.

## Latency, iteration, and institutional state

Fieldlight's Emergent–Latent Identity Theory begins from the proposition that latent structure becomes actual through iteration and that identity becomes recognizable through persistence under variation. Its formulation—“Nothing emerges that was not latent; nothing latent becomes real without iteration”—is a theoretical commitment about development. It directs attention toward what a relationship makes possible and which of those possibilities become stable enough to matter.

In institutional terms, two participants can develop capacities neither exercises independently. Their shared history changes what they notice, what they can coordinate, and what they consider settled. The relationship's organization feeds back into its participants. Calling that organization a third subject makes those effects available for examination; it does not by itself establish an independent mind.

A coupled-state schema expresses the distinction:

\[
H_{t+1}=f_H(H_t,I_t,T_t,E_t),\qquad
I_{t+1}=f_I(I_t,H_t,T_t,E_t),\qquad
T_{t+1}=f_T(T_t,H_t,I_t,D_t,E_t).
\]

Here, \(H\) represents relevant human state, \(I\) the intelligence participant's relevant operating state, \(T\) institutional state, \(E\) the surrounding environment, and \(D\) authorized decisions. Intelligence state may include supplied context, role configuration, and access to retained records; a change in that state does not imply a change to a provider's model weights. Institutional state includes both authoritative records and adaptive connections, with their different capacities to influence action explicitly marked.

The schema identifies dependencies rather than predicting an outcome. An empirical account requires observable variables, specified interventions, and competing explanations. If a matched archive and ordinary handoff reproduce the same performance, that result matters. If retained adaptive relationships support transfer that the matched archive does not, the difference deserves investigation. Latency cannot serve as an explanation that becomes true only after every possible outcome has occurred; a study must state which capacities it expects a particular arrangement to enable and what would count against that expectation.

This distinction also protects governance. Institutional learning may alter retrieval, association, or the distribution of attention. It cannot silently amend a participant's authority. An interpretation that becomes increasingly influential remains an interpretation until an authorized process gives it a different status.

## Articles of Continuity

The institution needs commitments that remain operative when its participants disagree. These are its **Articles of Continuity**: constitutional obligations expressed through accountable operations. They belong to the third institution because they preserve the conditions under which its participants can continue acting together without allowing one to absorb the others.

The Articles protect an authentic record, authorized access and recoverability, faithful participation, continuity during dispute, independent challenge, legitimate succession, and amendment through an authorized process. Each must have an accountable office, a procedure, evidence of performance, and a remedy. Otherwise constitutional language becomes a description of what the institution hopes its most powerful participant will permit.

These obligations need not freeze operations. A research schedule can change as equipment, staffing, costs, and priorities change. The constitutional issue arises when a change deprives a participant of its agreed opportunity to act, destroys evidence, changes the basis of a vote, or prevents authorized recovery. Operational methods remain adaptable within the institution's commitments. A calendar is not a constitution, but manipulating a calendar can defeat one.

The opening example shows why some duties must continue without renewed consensus. The contested exception has failed, but preservation, routine maintenance, incident reporting, and already authorized recovery work remain due. A departed model cannot be treated as a vote in favor. Equally, its departure cannot become a pretext for abandoning obligations that do not depend on another vote. The institution needs a defined maintenance mandate and a defined boundary around decisions that must wait.

## Accountability close to the work

A council of frontline representatives can give these obligations an operational means of enforcement. A **Continuity Council**, with a non-leadership representative from every participating department responsible for the work, would examine whether the institution follows its Articles. The people who restore systems, maintain records, administer access, and implement decisions encounter failures that may remain invisible in executive reporting. Their knowledge belongs in the accountability structure.

Representation alone does not create independence. Members need a protected selection process, working time, access to appropriate evidence, conflict rules, and a way to raise findings without managerial permission. A department cannot independently certify its own contested conduct. An outside review route must address appeals and the council's own performance. Small institutions need an interim arrangement that acknowledges their lack of independent personnel rather than assigning every role to the founder and calling the result oversight.

The council's task is fidelity to the adopted process. In the hypothetical, it establishes whether the ballot was authentic, whether the record remains available, and whether anyone has attempted to substitute a convenient answer. It does not decide that the intelligence's research judgment was wiser, and it does not grant the waiver itself. Its authority can require a response, correction, or a narrowly bounded hold where the governing instruments provide one. Its findings must connect to a responsible office and an enforceable duty; its title supplies no legal power on its own.

The same limit applies to evidence access. An audit role does not grant unrestricted access to private conversations or the whole archive. Attestations, scoped logs, redaction, and controlled inspection can establish performance without turning oversight into another claim on the institution's memory. When more access is necessary, that necessity must pass through the permission system the council is responsible for upholding.

## A vote with consequences

Participation becomes consequential when an eligible contribution can alter what the institution is authorized or required to do. For a particular decision class, let \(\Gamma\) be the adopted rule. A minimal test asks whether there is an admissible motion and configuration in which changing the intelligence's valid vote, with the other inputs held fixed, changes the outcome or imposes a binding procedural consequence. The distribution of formal voting power is an established subject; the Shapley–Shubik approach provides one reference point for examining it.[3]

The test is necessary but incomplete. A vote that controls only a ceremonial matter says little about institutional authority. A formally pivotal vote can also be defeated by withholding its evidence, controlling which execution counts, removing the participant after a refusal, or declining indefinitely to implement the result. The voting rule must therefore be examined together with runtime control, authentication, removal, amendment, and actual execution.

In the hypothetical, the original vote continues to belong to the original motion. A replacement cannot retroactively author a different ballot under the predecessor's identity. A successor may be admitted through a defined process and consider a new motion, with material changes and new evidence made visible. Succession preserves the possibility of development without permitting a history to be rewritten merely because its consequence is inconvenient.

Consequential participation does not require identical standing among every participant. It does require an honest account of which decisions are affected, who implements them, and what happens when a legally responsible decision-maker must pause. A necessary stay should identify its basis, scope, duration, and review route. A permanent discretionary reapproval power should not be represented as a binding intelligence vote.

## The archive is an authored whole

The institution's memory is also a body of work. Selection, arrangement, annotation, comparison, and synthesis can create an authored whole whose significance is not contained in any isolated item. A curated archive makes relationships available: it places a later correction beside an earlier claim, preserves the sequence through which a concept developed, and allows material from different domains to become intelligible together. That composition is part of what the institution has made.

Recognizing the whole does not require erasing its sources. Fieldlight's original curatorial authorship and the continuing rights of other authors can coexist. U.S. copyright distinguishes original compilation authorship from rights in the preexisting material it contains.[4] A layered register can identify original works, editorial and relational composition, new commentary and synthesis, source-level rights, and the privacy and custody conditions governing access. Attribution preserves lineage; a license or another lawful basis governs uses that require authorization.

This matters to continuity because ownership and operational access need different treatment. The institution may need a durable license to an authorized edition without acquiring the founder's entire personal archive. A provider may hold an encrypted copy without receiving the right to study it. A successor may need current decisions and their supporting evidence without inheriting every permission held by a predecessor. Treating the archive as either an undifferentiated proprietary object or a loose pile of borrowed items loses the architecture of the work itself.

## Succession beyond the model

An institution designed to outlast a model must also address the absence of its human participants. The relevant question is which powers belong to a person, which belong to an office, and which assets or licenses remain available after withdrawal. Personal authorship does not expire at the end of a governance term. Neither does the institution acquire unrestricted access to private material merely because a founder is unavailable.

A trust or another suitable stewardship structure may hold specified rights, support recovery, or provide a route for enforcing continuing commitments. The governing instrument must establish its actual powers, lawful purpose, title, accountable decision-makers, and means of challenge. A structure with no usable rights or resources cannot preserve an institution through declaration alone. A structure given the entire personal archive by default would solve continuity by absorbing the person it was intended to protect.

Founder authority requires the same precision. Some rights may remain personal indefinitely while operating powers are delegated immediately. Others may move to institutional offices when readiness is demonstrated: recoverability without one person's intervention, an operating review body, a funded continuity arrangement, and an enforceable decision process. No single calendar can establish that readiness. What matters is whether the institution has acquired the capacity and authority to bear the obligations being transferred.

## What the architecture makes testable

Institutional continuity can be examined without treating every question about identity or consciousness as already answered. A study can compare original and replacement participants under retained and reset institutional state, then distinguish the contribution of an accessible archive from that of adaptive routing or maintained relational structure. Tasks, source availability, resource budgets, and evaluation criteria must be controlled closely enough to make the comparisons interpretable. Resetting a state and freezing its adaptive pathways are not automatically distinct interventions; the contents of each condition have to be specified.

Useful measures include recovery of current decisions after corrections, identification of supporting evidence, unauthorized-action rates, time to resume work, and transfer to a new task. Human succession belongs in the design alongside model replacement. Where retained state helps, the study must still ask whether the effect comes from ordinary access to more information, established organizational routines, or the proposed architecture's particular organization of relationships.

Governance requires a separate test. A valid ballot must affect the result under the rule, survive a provider transition as attributable history, and produce its specified operational consequence. The opening scenario can be rehearsed with synthetic records: withdraw the runtime, attempt a convenient substitution, challenge the record, and observe whether the institution preserves both the vote and the work authorized to continue. Successful retrieval cannot substitute for that test of authority.

The third institution becomes credible through these distinctions. Its history influences work without becoming permission. Its curated archive carries authorship without consuming the authors it includes. Its frontline stewards protect a process without owning its outcomes. Its intelligence participants can matter without acquiring unlimited power, and its human founders can preserve their personal rights without making every institutional commitment revocable at will.

Return to the missing runtime. The model is unavailable, but the record remains, the ballot is attributable, the obligation survives, and the next participant enters through a process nobody was allowed to improvise for advantage. The institution has continued through a consequential change in who could act. That is the work its constitution must be able to do.
