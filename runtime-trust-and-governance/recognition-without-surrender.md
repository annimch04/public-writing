# Recognition Without Surrender
## Symbols, Memory, and Authority in Human–AI Collaboration

Anni McHenry

### 1. A flower that does intellectual work

A design draft from my Sanctum project, marked April 16, 2025, gives a memory system the anatomy of a flower. Its ovary held persistent memory; its petals made patterns recognizable; its stigma received signals and its stamens transmitted them. The stem routed information without becoming its permanent store. Mycelial roots extended the arrangement into memory between agents.[1] These assignments made a problem available to thought: how could work continue when a conversation ended, a visible interface disappeared, or a different participant took over?

The flower was a way of reasoning about that problem. Petals could fall while the core survived. Reception could be separated from transmission. Something could carry information without owning its durable record. Thinking through the image made it possible to ask which functions should remain together and which required separate treatment. The representation did not have to be an executable system to make those questions more precise.

Its usefulness also produced a difficulty. A symbol can gather relationships into a form that is easier to recognize than to explain. When an AI participates in interpreting it, recognition may allow the collaboration to advance before every relationship has been explicitly stated. But what, exactly, has that recognition established? A recovered context? An understanding of a proposed design? A permission to act?

The question is present within the retained documents themselves. A loss-resolution draft treats recognition as permission. An invocation policy, filed April 17, requires an authorization signal and rejects permission inferred merely from familiarity or perceived intent.[2] This is a substantive tension within an architecture being developed. It gives us an opportunity to distinguish things that fluent collaboration can make appear identical.

I argue that symbolic recognition can support discovery and continuity without, by itself, authorizing action. More precisely, the grounds for interpreting a symbol do not automatically establish the standing to use it as a directive. A symbol can perform both functions, but the transition requires an account of the practice in which it operates. That distinction lets us preserve the generative value of symbolic work while examining who determines what follows from it. The argument must also address encounters in which meanings and capacities develop that no participant specified in advance. I will argue that emergence can transform the terms of a collaboration without making its stable patterns self-authorizing.

### 2. From image to a reasoned proposal

An image does not need to contain a hidden finished proposition to help someone think. It can make a relationship salient, invite an inference, or expose a conflict among purposes. In the Flower, separating the stem from the ovary suggests a distinction between transporting information and keeping it. Once articulated, that distinction can be tested against a proposed system: does a routing component retain a copy, and is that retention part of its assigned role? The image supports the formulation of a question that can subsequently receive a more exact answer.

This matters to how discovery is described. If the only credited intellectual act is the final explicit statement, the work of constructing and interpreting the representation disappears from the account. Yet the statement may become available through that work. Formalization can clarify, constrain, and sometimes overturn what an image suggests. It need not be the first moment at which reasoning occurs.

The reverse movement matters too. Discovering that a routing component must temporarily retain data may require refining the original separation. A diagram changes in response to its specification, just as a specification changes in response to a diagram. The useful relation is therefore not a ladder from vague symbolism to finished technical thought. It is a movement between representations with different capacities for exposing relations and testing their consequences.

There is a straightforward objection: analogies encourage illicit transfers. Because a flower grows, a designer may suppose the memory system will grow in a corresponding way. Because roots connect living things, the designer may attribute understanding to a network that only passes references. The objection identifies a responsibility within symbolic reasoning. A proposed correspondence must be distinguished from an established property of the system. Assigning routing to a stem helps identify a function; it does not demonstrate that routing has been implemented.

The objection does not remove the image's cognitive contribution. A model can be useful without licensing all the inferences its source domain suggests. Its intellectual value depends on the relationships selected, the consequences investigated, and the revisions made when the correspondence fails. The Flower combines botanical anatomy with a fungal network because the design problem requires memory beyond one participant. That combination is an authored arrangement whose adequacy depends on what it enables us to explain, rather than fidelity to a single natural organism.

### 3. Recognition is an achievement, but which one?

Suppose a collaborator encounters the phrase “the petals have fallen.” In the context of this architecture, they might understand it to mean that the visible presentation has disappeared while a persistent source remains. They might identify the relevant record, propose a recovery procedure, or infer which part of the system failed. These are different achievements, even when they occur in one fluent response.

A further step would be to restore a particular record as the current state and continue acting from it. That step raises a question the interpretation alone cannot settle: which source is entitled to govern continuation? The most recognizable record might be an abandoned design. The newest might be an experiment rather than an adopted revision. A response that reconstructs the relationships accurately can still assign the wrong practical status to what it recovers.

Three distinctions are useful here. **Interpretation** concerns what a symbol means in a context. **Epistemic warrant** concerns the grounds for accepting the interpretation or drawing an inference from it. **Authorization** concerns what someone may do on that basis. A person may understand a confidential letter, have excellent evidence about its meaning, and still lack permission to publish it. Likewise, an assistant may understand a symbolic instruction from an earlier project without being entitled to reactivate it in a later one.

These distinctions should not be confused with a demand for literal language. Ordinary words can be ambiguous; a symbol can be precisely established. The relevant question is how a representation acquires its role. A flower can be an exploratory model in one encounter, the name of an architecture in another, and a reference to an agreed recovery procedure in a third. Continuity of expression does not guarantee continuity of function.

In particular, recognition can be evidence that a convention has been understood. It cannot alone establish that a convention was adopted. Otherwise the interpreter could confer authority on its own reading merely by recognizing it. The person's ability to offer a possibility without instituting a rule would disappear.

### 4. When a symbol really does authorize

An objection now becomes pressing. Human practices routinely use symbols to authorize action. A signature, an agreed signal, or a command can do more than describe a situation. Why deny that power to a recognition phrase developed with an AI?

There is no reason to deny it. The invocation policy itself allows a ritual phrase with an established system role.[2] Its significance is that the phrase is situated within a practice that gives it authority. A person might establish that a particular signal permits a collaborator to restore the last approved working state. Recognizing the signal then enables the collaborator to act under a standing delegation. The permission need not be laboriously reconstructed each time.

Consider two cases with the same phrase. In one, the person uses it while brainstorming how continuity might work. In the other, the person establishes it as the trigger for a defined recovery procedure. An assistant can recognize the phrase correctly in both cases. What distinguishes them is the act or practice that makes the second occurrence operative. The phrase's familiarity cannot stand in for that history.

Nor is explicit registration the only way a convention can arise. Repeated, mutually understood practice can establish a permission. But that explanation requires evidence of the practice and its scope. Repetition of words alone is insufficient: a person can repeatedly discuss a proposal without adopting it. Apparent agreement also needs to be evaluated against correction, withdrawal, and the different roles participants occupy.

Consider a third case, in which no one formally registers the phrase. A researcher repeatedly uses it when asking a collaborator to recover a working document. The collaborator restores the document, identifies which version it has selected, and receives corrections when that selection is wrong. Over time, both participants organize their work around that use. There is evidence of a convention here because the phrase coordinates conduct and because the corrections establish what counts as following it. The same phrase appearing repeatedly in speculative discussion would not supply that evidence.

This is a defeasible basis for action, not a requirement to ask the same question forever. Once a practice establishes recovery of the last approved draft, a collaborator may perform that recovery without a fresh grant on each occasion. Yet permission to recover a draft does not itself authorize publishing it or replacing it with a preferred revision. The convention gives a reason for some actions, and its scope supplies a reason against others. A capable collaborator must infer both.

Suppose the researcher later says that the phrase should now retrieve the history for comparison while leaving the current draft untouched. The earlier convention remains part of the history, but its former practical force has changed. A memory that preserves only the repeated phrase and the old recovery procedure would preserve evidence of a real permission while losing evidence of its revision. The failure would not be invention of a permission that never existed. It would be the continued exercise of one that no longer governs the present task.

These cases suggest three questions for assessing an inherited convention: what conduct did the participants establish, what limits did their uptake and corrections give it, and what later decisions changed its force? They do not amount to a complete theory of legitimate authority. They identify evidence needed to explain why this representation licenses this action now. Mere frequency of occurrence cannot answer them, and silence alone need not count as adoption. Equally, the absence of a formal registration does not erase a practice that the participants have demonstrably established.

The central claim can therefore be stated without excluding symbolic authorization: **recognition permits the exercise of authority only where there is an independently justified basis for treating what is recognized as operative in that context.** “Independently” does not mean unrelated to the interaction. It means that the interpreter's confidence is not itself the whole justification. A grant, an established convention, or a legitimate role can provide the missing basis.

This distinction protects the possibility of creative exploration. Someone can use a symbol to think with a system without every fruitful interpretation becoming a commitment. They can also deliberately make an interpretation binding. The difference is essential to collaboration because exploration and commitment are both things people need to do.

### 5. A memory can preserve meaning while changing its force

The difficulty becomes more acute across encounters. Suppose a first system retains the following summary: “The Flower represents continuity after the visible interface is lost.” A later system uses that summary to locate prior work. So far, the memory serves interpretation and retrieval. Suppose instead that it treats any record bearing the Flower's name as the state from which future work must proceed. A descriptive association has become a rule for selecting authority.

The summary need not be false. Its accuracy would not answer the objection. The person might affirm every descriptive word while rejecting the practical consequence. A mechanism that allows only factual correction—marking a memory true or false—would not capture the difference between “this expresses my design” and “this authorizes you to install that version.”

Nissenbaum's account of contextual integrity evaluates information practices through context-specific norms of appropriateness and distribution.[3] The present question concerns a particular transformation within continuing collaboration: the role assigned to information after it has been retained or passed along. Appropriate access to a record does not settle which of its contents may govern action. The question can arise even when the same collaborators retain the information within the same project: what standing should that information have in the next action? This is a question about the use of a retained record as a reason for acting; I do not assume that contextual integrity excludes it.

My argument in *Local Authority, External Capability* concerns the terms of dependence on systems that provide capabilities beyond the person's own.[4] Symbolic memory makes one of those terms especially clear. A person can benefit from a collaborator's ability to recover meaning without delegating the power to determine which interpretation becomes canonical. Dependence on recognition need not entail surrender of the distinction between a proposed meaning and an adopted instruction.

A useful record must therefore preserve more than content. It must make its role recoverable: proposal, interpretation, example, approved instruction, superseded instruction, or some other status appropriate to the practice. These categories are not necessarily permanent. A proposal can become an instruction. What matters is that the change have an intelligible basis and remain open to the appropriate kind of challenge.

### 6. The archive as a philosophical research resource

The materials behind this case belong to a longitudinal archive containing both artifacts and records of the interactions through which some of them developed. Drafts, diagrams, code, and published versions can be compared with requests, interpretations, corrections, and subsequent decisions. The collection makes it possible to investigate the movement between representations rather than examine a polished account alone.[5]

This is important because a retrospective explanation can make a developing concept look settled from the beginning. The loss-resolution draft closes by treating recognition as permission; the invocation policy instead specifies direct instructions, authorized ritual phrases, and visible permission toggles. It also rejects permission inferred from familiarity or perceived intent.[2] Read together, these documents expose a question about the conditions under which recognition has practical force. Their coexistence supplies the case for this argument without requiring a claim that one document superseded the other. Establishing a historical revision would require the interaction and adoption records as well as the documents themselves.

The unit of inquiry is consequently a connected sequence: an expression in context, an interpretation of it, the role assigned to that interpretation, and a resulting artifact or action where one survives. Comparing those elements can distinguish an assistant's claim to have preserved a constraint from a revision that actually does preserve it. It can also show productive transformations: an image becomes a functional distinction, a distinction becomes a design requirement, and a test sends the collaboration back to revise the image.

A surviving reorientation helper illustrates why these comparisons matter. The code scans a memory index backward for a heading beginning with a date prefix, constructs a memory-file pointer from that heading, and writes the pointer into a state file.[6] It does not check an approval status before making that selection. That is a particular recovery operation. It is not interchangeable with the broader symbolic account of recognition. Keeping the design and the component together gives research a way to ask which proposed relationships acquired an implementation and which remained open. Neither the prose nor the code has to stand in for the other.

The archive is thus an asset because it preserves transformations and opportunities for comparison. Its value does not depend on every sequence supporting the same conclusion. Abandoned symbols, rejected interpretations, and discrepancies between description and artifact can reveal where an account needs revision. The present argument uses one documented tension to distinguish normative relations; a broader study could investigate how frequently those relations are preserved or collapsed across the surviving encounters.

My participation gives access to the purposes and corrections through which the work developed. It also makes it necessary to retain the difference between contemporaneous records and my present interpretation. Successive models are participants in the history, and later model-generated summaries are additional interpretations of it. They do not become independent corroboration merely by agreeing. A research account should let a reader follow those relationships without requiring acceptance of a single retrospective story.

### 7. An encounter can change what there is to recognize

The account so far might seem to assign collaboration a limited role. A person establishes a meaning or grants a permission; a system recognizes it and acts within its scope. Yet some of the most valuable encounters begin before the person knows what to establish. An image becomes a distinction through the attempt to explain it. An objection changes the question rather than merely its answer. The work can produce a possibility that neither participant could have specified before taking part. An account that protects only previously articulated intentions would leave this contribution unexplained.

My public work approaches this problem through several forms. In *Architecture of Becoming*, recurrence changes the conditions of the next return. *Emergent–Latent Identity Theory* distinguishes a range of possibilities from the particular form that develops through interaction. *Permeability and Agency* locates agency partly in changing one's responsiveness and the conditions of encounter.[7] These approaches make a common demand: explain continuity without assuming that what continues must remain unchanged. A person can be the source of a project whose development changes what she understands herself to be doing.

There is a tension within this account worth making explicit. If everything that emerges was latent, what has the interaction contributed? One answer would treat the finished result as already contained within a participant, awaiting extraction. But a range of possible developments does not contain a decision about which relations to establish or which consequences to pursue. The eventual arrangement depends on the encounter. To say that a distinction was possible before a conversation is not to say that either participant already possessed it, or that its particular formulation was inevitable. Here I use emergence to identify that relational achievement, without making the stronger claim that it is inexplicable in terms of the processes that produce it.

*Theory of Signal Becoming* gives this movement a more specific structure: a partial expression returns through different encounters, changes through uptake and correction, and acquires a form that can be deliberately stated and revised.[8] The distinction between development and repetition is essential. A sentence can recur because it has proved useful under criticism, because an interface keeps presenting it, or because later summaries copy an earlier mistake. All three can produce familiarity. Only the first, as described, supplies evidence that the sentence has survived the relevant challenge. Stability needs an account of its production.

The same requirement applies to authority. A representation may become clearer, more useful, and more widely recognized without becoming an instruction. Its development gives participants something new to assess; it does not settle the assessment. Equally, a convention may emerge through practice without anyone having formulated it in advance. The task is to distinguish these developments within the interaction, rather than divide the world into an authorized past and an unauthorized future.

De Jaegher and Di Paolo's account of participatory sense-making provides a useful interlocutor. They argue that interaction can acquire an organization that contributes to the generation and transformation of meaning, while the individuals remain autonomous participants. Their example of two people repeatedly moving into each other's path illustrates how an interaction can sustain itself despite their attempts to end it. The continuing pattern cannot be explained simply as the execution of a shared intention to continue.[9, pp. 492–494] This supports attention to the organization of an encounter alongside its participants' contributions. Their account of autonomy concerns the maintenance of an organization; it should not simply be equated with a right to authorize another's action. I use this distinction to examine human–AI collaboration, without assuming that an AI meets their criteria for an autonomous participant.

That distinction matters for my argument. Participation can change what a person means without giving the resulting process authority over her next decision. Suppose I develop a new interpretation while working with a collaborator. The interpretation genuinely depends on that encounter. I nevertheless remain able to judge it mistaken, suspend it, or pursue a different consequence. Denying those possibilities would make the success of the encounter a reason to exempt its product from criticism. The more deeply an interaction changed my thinking, the less standing I would have to question what it produced. That reverses the intellectual value of collaboration.

This does not restore an isolated individual who creates meanings alone. It locates a normative question inside a process of mutual change. How an interpretation arose, who contributed to it, whether it is defensible, and what uses are permitted remain distinguishable questions even when they concern one developing work. Their answers can constrain one another without being interchangeable. Acknowledging a collaborator's contribution, for example, does not require endorsing every conclusion the collaboration reached.

### 8. The medium can preserve a discovery or manufacture a default

Consider a thought experiment extending the Flower case. A researcher and an assistant develop a diagram for recovering work across interrupted conversations. Initially, every piece of retained material is represented by the same kind of petal. The assistant repeatedly groups rejected alternatives with current proposals because the diagram does not distinguish their status. The researcher separates those groups. Subsequent attempts reveal that a rejected alternative can still be useful evidence of why the current design has its form. Together they develop a representation with two independently changeable features: availability for comparison and authority to govern continuation.

This distinction is an outcome of the work. Neither participant began by specifying it. The diagram's inadequacy, the assistant's groupings, and the researcher's corrections each contributed to making it available. A record that described the result as the execution of the researcher's original detailed instruction would lose the discovery. A record that attributed it solely to the assistant's final formulation would lose the problem and corrections through which that formulation became possible. Authorship requires an account of the transformation, including the labor of identifying what an apparently successful response got wrong.

Now suppose the workspace remembers which materials were used most frequently. Its ranking changes as the pair work. Frequently selected passages appear first; neglected alternatives become harder to retrieve. This medium does more than carry their messages. It changes the encounter by distributing attention and making some continuations easier than others. The participants can make a real discovery through it even if they do not understand every feature of its ranking. The philosophical question is what happens when the same mechanism treats repeated selection as evidence of authorization.

Compare two versions of this workspace. In the first, frequency changes the ordering of suggestions, while an independently maintained status distinguishes material available for discussion from material adopted for execution. In the second, a frequently selected passage automatically becomes the default instruction for later assistants. During exploratory work, the visible conversations could be nearly identical. Both systems might recover useful passages and support the new distinction. Yet when another assistant continues the project, the second workspace can exercise a permission that the participants never established. Its success in supporting discovery does not justify that transition.

My *Fascia as Infrastructure* draft asks what a responsive medium contributes to a network, including what happens when its history is preserved, reset, or made unable to adapt.[10] Those comparisons clarify the thought experiment. Replacing the assistant while retaining the workspace could reveal that a pattern depends on shared history rather than that particular assistant. Resetting the rankings could reveal how presentation shapes recurrence. Keeping the same rankings while changing the rules for adoption could expose the difference between making a passage likely to be encountered and making it entitled to direct action. These are proposed comparisons, not results of experiments already conducted.

The case also explains why protecting agency requires more than a final approval button. If a workspace systematically hides alternatives or treats objections as irrelevant, the person may formally approve a result while lacking practical access to the history needed to assess it. Conversely, an interface can preserve every alternative and overwhelm the person so thoroughly that correction becomes unusable. The relevant capacity is neither exhaustive exposure nor constant interruption. It is the ability to recover consequential distinctions when they matter, understand what an action would commit, and make a correction that changes subsequent conduct.

This develops the account of selective permeability in my earlier work. A boundary can regulate an encounter without closing it. The researcher can invite the assistant to introduce new distinctions while restricting which records it may publish, replace, or treat as binding. These permissions operate at different levels. Openness to conceptual change does not entail unlimited discretion over the material conditions of the project. A capable system should be able to work within that combination rather than interpret every restriction as a rejection of collaboration.

The strongest practical test would therefore involve disagreement. When the researcher rejects the apparent default, does the workspace retain the discovery while revising its practical status? Can it preserve an abandoned interpretation as part of the history without presenting it again as her current commitment? A system that can do both supports development through correction. A system that can only preserve or erase forces a false choice between keeping what the encounter made possible and retaining authority over what follows from it.

### 9. Becoming does not require surrendering the right to revise

An objection remains. If participants could withdraw whenever a collaboration changed them, shared work would become impossible. A project depends on expectations that persist beyond each moment's preference. A person can make a commitment whose consequences she did not fully anticipate. Why should novelty reopen what an established practice already settled?

The objection is right about commitment, but it does not show that every stable pattern is one. We need to identify the relevant undertaking and its scope. Agreeing that a collaborator may select a recovery method differs from agreeing that every successful recovery method will govern future projects. Agreeing to develop an argument differs from agreeing to publish the eventual result. An unforeseen outcome can fall within a valid delegation; its novelty alone does not invalidate that delegation. What requires justification is the expansion from the authorized activity to a further authority claimed on the basis of its success.

Nor does revision give a participant unilateral control over every consequence of shared work. A person cannot erase another's contribution merely by withdrawing her endorsement. She may owe an explanation, preserve attribution, or satisfy commitments on which others reasonably relied. These obligations must be argued for in their own terms. My claim is narrower: the causal dependence of a result on an interaction does not, by itself, establish an obligation to continue that interaction or accept its current direction. Dependence explains how something became possible. An undertaking or another appropriate normative ground must explain what is owed.

This matters especially when the interaction itself has acquired momentum. A rhythm of rapid agreement can make a pause feel like a failure of collaboration. Repeatedly productive suggestions can make rejection seem ungrateful. A shared vocabulary can make alternatives difficult to express without appearing to abandon the project altogether. None of these conditions makes the work worthless. They explain why productive participation and the practical ability to dissent need to be assessed together. The question is whether disagreement remains capable of changing the relation, not merely whether the person can insert the word no into it.

There is a corresponding objection from the assistant's side. Requiring recoverable authority for every useful inference might return us to the paralysis this paper rejects. In the thought experiment, should the assistant have stopped before suggesting a distinction the researcher had not requested? No. An invitation to develop the diagram can authorize exploratory contributions whose contents are unspecified. The assistant can exercise judgment by producing, testing, and criticizing possibilities. What it cannot infer from that invitation alone is permission to make every useful possibility the standing rule of the workspace. Initiative is sustained by interpreting the actual scope of the invitation, including its openness.

There will be difficult cases where the scope is tacit or unsettled. Their difficulty is a reason to make the competing readings available, especially when acting would close options or establish continuing commitments. The person can then broaden the delegation, endorse the interpretation, or revise the project. Such decisions are themselves part of the intellectual work. Others retain their own standing to refuse participation or impose justified conditions; preserving the researcher's authority does not make every consequence hers to determine.

A further distinction follows between changing a participant's capacities and determining their commitments. The researcher may become able to notice a problem she could not previously formulate. She cannot simply return to a condition in which she never encountered it. Yet she can still decide that one proposed solution is inadequate. The irreversibility of learning does not make its first formulation binding. To equate those would give an interaction the power to determine a person's obligations merely by affecting her deeply enough. Intellectual development would become a trap: learning more would reduce the freedom to assess what one had learned.

The account of recognition can now be strengthened. The relevant authority need not precede every encounter in a fully specified form. It can develop through reciprocal practice, including the correction of expectations neither participant initially articulated. But its development must remain distinguishable from the stabilization of a pattern. That distinction requires attention to how participants establish scope, answer objections, and revise what their conduct permits. A convention can genuinely emerge; calling something emergent does not establish that a convention has emerged.

In *Before I Had a Proposition*, I describe the conversation as changing the material it records.[11] The consequence reaches beyond the accuracy of a later summary. A record of an interaction can help constitute the next one by selecting which meanings, corrections, and expectations remain available. The archive therefore has a double role: evidence of how a collaboration developed, and part of the infrastructure through which its development continues. Preserving that distinction makes it possible to inspect the archive's effects rather than treat its mere survival as proof of faithful continuity.

The resulting ideal is demanding but not contradictory. Participants should be able to create more together than they could specify separately, while retaining differentiated standing over the consequences. A project can preserve the contribution that changed it, acknowledge that its participants changed, and still leave its next commitment unsettled. The freedom at issue is not immunity from influence. It is the practical and normative possibility of taking part in what one becomes without treating every change as a surrender of authority.

### 10. What should continue

The Flower's central ambition was to let an encounter end without making the work begin again. Symbols contribute to that ambition by making relationships recognizable across changes of form. Their value includes the capacity to expose a relation before it has a settled formulation, and to carry it into another encounter where it can be developed further.

That capacity also makes their practical status consequential. A system that inherits a symbol may inherit a proposal, a memory, a convention, or a command. It must not flatten those differences simply because the symbol remains familiar. The archive helps make them visible by preserving how expressions were used, interpreted, adopted, and corrected.

Continuity should preserve the possibility of revision, including when the collaboration has changed what its participants can recognize and do. The medium that sustains a discovery must also make it possible to challenge the authority assigned to that discovery. It should not merely reproduce a recognizable pattern. A person can want to be understood and still be deciding what follows from that understanding. Human–AI collaboration becomes more capable when a shared symbol opens that decision to further thought. It exceeds its authority when recognition is treated as having already made the decision.
